Macroeconomic Environment During the third quarter of 2026, the Chilean economy operated in a challenging external environment. The conflict in the Middle East kept fuel prices high, especially for refined products, while higher food and freight costs put pressure on the prices of imported goods. Major central banks reinforced their restrictive stance in the face of resilient global activity and the risk of the energy shock being passed on to underlying inflation. In this context, local long-term interest rates rose in line with those in the United States. Even so, the sovereign spread remained narrow, and copper continued to be a significant support for the external accounts. Domestically, activity remained weak. The Imacec (Monthly Index of Economic Activity) fell 1.0% YoY and 0.7% month-on-month in August, seasonally adjusted, mainly due to a 17.4% YoY contraction in mining, which reached its lowest level since 1996. The non-mining component rebounded 0.7% month-on-month, but only reversed the decline of July and remained practically stagnant compared to the end of 2015. In this context, we project zero growth for 2026, with risks still skewed to the downside by potential climate disruptions and transitory shocks that continue to affect mining production. For 2027, we anticipate an expansion of 3.5%, supported by the normalization of the mining sector, a low base of comparison, and greater implementation of large investment projects, mainly in mining and energy. This is further bolstered by the effect that the Reconstruction Law is expected to begin to take hold. Currently, some leading indicators, such as non- energy imports and perceived demand for credit, are beginning to show early signs of increased dynamism, although still insufficient to confirm a widespread recovery in domestic demand. The labor market showed a deterioration during 2026, in line with the weakness in economic activity. Slower formal job creation and low wage growth limited household spending. We expect the job recovery to be gradual and to lag behind the increased dynamism of economic activity, supported by project implementation. In terms of prices, the CPI rose 0.4% month-on-month in September, one-tenth of a percentage point below expectations, and annual inflation remained at 4.1%. Pressures continued to be concentrated in volatile components, especially energy and food, while the CPI excluding volatile items fell to 3.2% annually, consistent with weak domestic demand. Nevertheless, high international fuel prices and the depreciation of the peso are expected to push inflation up to 5.5% by the end of 2026, before converging back toward the target during the following year. The divergence between higher headline inflation and contained core inflation reduces the need to respond to the shock with an immediate rate hike. We therefore maintain our policy rate projection at 4.5% through 2027. The balance of risks, however, is tilted toward a more restrictive path if the energy shock is prolonged, if the exchange rate remains depreciated, or if two-year inflation expectations begin to deviate from 3%. 11

Advances in Risk-Weighted Asset (RWA) models The implementation of Basel III in Chile continues to progress through adjustments to Risk-Weighted Asset (RWA) models, focusing on market risk and credit risk. These changes aim to ensure that capital requirements more accurately reflect the risks assumed by banks, bringing local regulations closer to international standards and improving comparability with other jurisdictions. Regarding market risk, on September 11, 2026, the CMF published the final regulation incorporating the duration method as an alternative for measuring overall interest rate risk in determining Market Risk-Weighted Assets (MRWA). This approach allows for a more accurate capture of the sensitivity of positions to interest rate movements. Its adoption is voluntary and requires CMF approval, for which entities must demonstrate proper implementation through methodological, control, and internal validation documentation. According to previous CMF estimates, this modification would reduce the system's MRWA by approximately 36%. Additionally, the regulation continues to advance in defining criteria for the exclusion and offsetting of derivatives positions, with the aim of better reflecting the effects of hedging in the calculation of MRWA. Regarding credit risk, in August the CMF launched a public consultation on a new regulatory framework that consolidates and refines the requirements for adopting internal models for calculating provisions and risk- weighted assets. The proposal includes foundational and advanced methodologies. It also requires that the initial application cover at least 20% of modelable exposures and establishes a five-year timeframe for achieving full coverage, meaning its implementation would be phased in. Law of National Reconstruction and Economic and Social Development On October 17, 2026, Law No. 21,850 on National Reconstruction and Economic and Social Development was published, with measures to be implemented gradually between November 2026 and 2029. Among its main components is a tax agenda aimed at strengthening investment, improving competitiveness, and supporting economic recovery. The law includes a gradual reduction of the First Category Tax rate from 27% to 25.5% in 2027, 24% in 2028, and 23% from 2029 onward. Furthermore, starting in 2027, the tax system will be fully restructured, simplifying the tax structure, reducing the cost of capital, and increasing incentives for private investment. Furthermore, starting December 1, 2026, and for a period of twelve months, the first sale of new homes with final municipal approval will be exempt from VAT. This measure aims to accelerate the absorption of available housing stock, stimulate the real estate and construction sectors, and facilitate access to housing. Additionally, the law includes incentives for formal employment, investment benefits, property tax exemptions for senior citizens, and the creation of a special fund for the reconstruction of areas affected by wildfires. Mortgage Subsidy Law On May 20, 2025, Congress approved the Mortgage Subsidy Law to facilitate access to homeownership and revitalize the real estate market. This initiative includes a subsidy of up to 60 basis points on mortgage interest rates. Furthermore, it is backed by a state guarantee from the Special Guarantee Fund (FOGAES) that covers up to 60% of the property value, which could further reduce the total interest rate by up to 100 basis points. 13
The project aimed to provide 50,000 subsidies, 10% of which were earmarked for first-time homebuyers valued up to 3,000 UF (Unidades de Fomento, a Chilean unit of account) and the remaining percentage for new homes valued up to 4,000 UF. In 2026, the program was expanded, increasing the number of available subsidies from 50,000 to 80,000 and raising the maximum value of eligible homes from 4,000 UF to 6,000 UF. To date, this program has been a significant factor in supporting the demand for mortgage loans. According to ABIF (Chilean Banking Association), as of September 25, 2026, 113,781 eligible applications had been received and commercially approved, and more than 33,033 mortgages had been processed. Pension Reform On January 29, 2025, Congress approved the pension reform after a lengthy process of considering various proposals. The main objective is to increase future and current pensions, taking into account the following changes. Pension contributions will increase by 7%, paid by employers, starting at 1% in August 2025 and gradually increasing over a period of 9 to 11 years. Of the total percentage, 6% will go to direct, deferred individual capitalization, and 1% to the new Social Security system, which will also include the current 1.5% allocated to Disability and Survivors' Insurance. These resources will allow for equal pensions for men and women in similar circumstances at the time of retirement. The reform also includes changes to the pension fund structure. The multi-fund system will be replaced by generational funds, and new administrators will be allowed to enter the system. A 10% share of the existing pension fund will be put out to tender every two years to increase competition. In addition, the reform proposes increasing the Guaranteed Universal Pension (PGU) to $250,000 pesos for the 90% of the most vulnerable population. More than a year after the law's enactment, the implementation of the pension reform continues to progress gradually according to the legal timeline. Significant milestones have been reached during 2026, such as the launch of the Single Contribution Collection System (SUCC), the commencement of pre-litigation collection by the General Treasury of the Republic, and the implementation of the new Social Security contributions. Furthermore, a new phase of increased employer contributions began in August, while in September the Guaranteed Universal Pension (PGU) for individuals aged 75 and over increased. Several operational measures have already been implemented. Among these, mandates have already been awarded for the administration of the autonomous pension protection funds (FAPP), and the publication of the Investment Regime for Generational Funds defines the investment structure, risk limits, and benchmark portfolios of the funds that will replace the multi-funds starting in April 2027. Beyond the details, it is commendable that the reform allows for a gradual recovery of pension savings levels. This is beneficial not only for financing future pensions but also for the capital market, even though the final impact will depend on the implementation of the new investment rules of the new system. 14

Consolidated debt In early June 2024, the law creating the Consolidated Debt Registry (REDEC) was passed. This registry consolidates information on outstanding loans granted to individuals. The registry is public and free of charge, and is managed by the Financial Market Commission (CMF). The legislation expands the scope of entities required to report, incorporating, in addition to banks and savings and credit cooperatives, mortgage loan administrators, compensation funds, credit card issuers regulated by the CMF, securitization companies, credit advisory entities regulated by the Fintec Law, and other entities that the CMF may determine through a General Regulation. Access to registry information will generally require the prior, express, and unequivocal consent of the debtor, granted for the assessment of their credit risk in a specific transaction and for a limited period, unless there is another legal basis for access under Law No. 19,628 or the information is anonymized. In July 2025, the CMF issued the REDEC operational framework, defining reporting obligations, applicable procedures, and information security and quality standards. The regulations came into effect in April 2026, and since then the CMF has issued technical adjustments to the Information System Manual, resolved queries from reporting entities, and continued to incorporate new actors required to report. Regulatory priority has focused on stabilizing the system and ensuring the quality of reported information, a key element for proper credit risk assessment and the functioning of the financial market. Fintech Law and Open Finance System The Open Finance System (SFA) originated from the Fintec Law (Law No. 21,521), published in January 2023, which established the legal framework for the secure and consensual exchange of financial information. In July 2024, the CMF issued NCG No. 514, which regulates the SFA, defines the participation obligations of regulated entities and the voluntary registration of new providers, and set its entry into force for July 2026. This timeframe raised concerns about operational feasibility, implementation costs, and the maturity of the technical standards. 15
Therefore, between July 2025 and February 2026, the CMF conducted three public consultations to adjust the schedule and detail the system's technological standards, including payment initiation. The main recent milestone was the issuance of the final standard NCG No. 569, which contains the technical specifications for system interoperability and introduces a series of adjustments aimed at facilitating a gradual and secure implementation of the SFA. These include postponing the effective date until July 2027, incorporating a pilot period for controlled testing among participants, establishing a technological sandbox managed by the CMF, creating simplified participation mechanisms for smaller entities, and standardizing procedures to reduce operational friction. Despite these advances, key regulatory aspects remain to be defined, including the cost-sharing model between entities that provide information and those that consume it. In this context, the SFA remains a regulatory project under development, and its final design will be crucial in assessing its impact on competition, innovation, and compliance costs for financial institutions in Chile. In this context, in December 2025, the Central Bank authorized a local fintech company to operate a low-value payment clearinghouse, enabling potential future changes in the functioning of the payments market. Personal Data Protection Law In December 2024, a law was published modernizing the protection and processing of personal data in Chile, establishing a regulatory framework aligned with international standards such as those of the European Union. The legislation creates the Personal Data Protection Agency, the body responsible for overseeing compliance with the law, imposing sanctions, issuing guidelines, and promoting best practices in data processing. The law recognizes new rights for data subjects—access, rectification, erasure, objection, and portability—and establishes relevant obligations for organizations, such as implementing compliance programs, adopting security measures, managing incidents, and appointing a Data Protection Officer. Currently, the law is still scheduled to come into effect on December 1, 2026. However, in September 2026, the Government submitted a bill proposing to postpone its entry into force to December 1, 2027, and strengthen the structure of the Data Protection Agency. As of October 19, 2026, this amendment is still under legislative review, so the current legal date remains December 2026. Guarantee management as credit risk mitigators In July 2026, the Financial Market Commission published Circular No. 2,373, which establishes a new prudential framework for the management, valuation, and recognition of collateral used by banks as credit risk mitigators. The regulation incorporates Chapter 21-10 of the Updated Compilation of Regulations and reinforces the responsibility of the Board of Directors and senior management regarding the policies, controls, and procedures applicable throughout the collateral lifecycle. The circular requires that guarantees be legally valid, enforceable, properly documented, and prudently valued, taking into account their liquidity, execution costs and timeframes, asset volatility, and market conditions. It also establishes obligations for periodic revaluation, traceability, safekeeping, independent monitoring, and recording of amounts actually recovered. Guarantees that do not meet these conditions may be withdrawn to reduce credit exposure, leading to higher provisions and potentially greater capital consumption. 16
Most of the provisions will come into effect on January 1, 2028. However, the changes related to the assessment of the quality of guarantee management by the CMF are effective immediately, so banks must begin adapting their policies, valuation methodologies, information systems, controls and execution processes in advance. Capital Markets Reform On September 9, 2026, the Executive Branch submitted to Congress the Capital Market Reform and Homeownership Financing Bill, which includes more than 30 measures organized into four pillars: access to housing and promotion of savings; financing for companies of different sizes; modernization of financial market regulations and processes; and strengthening Chile as a regional financial center. Among its main measures are the creation of the National Housing Fund (FONAVI), with government contributions of up to US$2 billion, intended to finance mortgage loans for first-time homebuyers up to 6,000 UF (Unidades de Fomento). The bill also includes Voluntary Housing Savings (AVV), which supplements down payment savings with a government subsidy; the creation of a Junior Segment on the Stock Exchange for emerging and mining exploration companies; and tax adjustments to financial instruments. On October 27, the Chamber of Deputies approved the bill, which now begins its second constitutional review in the Senate. Regulation and supervision In Chile, only banks can maintain checking accounts for their clients, conduct foreign trade operations, and, along with regulated non-bank financial institutions such as cooperatives, accept time deposits. The main authorities regulating financial institutions in Chile are the Financial Market Commission (CMF) and the Central Bank. Chilean banks are primarily subject to the General Banking Law and, secondarily, to the extent that it is not incompatible with this law, to the provisions of the Chilean Corporations Law governing corporations, except for certain provisions that expressly exclude them.
