Q3 2026 and YTD Review
Sprott’s Assets Under Management (“AUM”) were $57.3 billion as at September 30, 2026, up 3% from $55.6 billion as at June 30, 2026 and down 4% from $59.6 billion as of December 31, 2025. On a three-month ended basis, our AUM was positively impacted by market value appreciation across a majority of our fund products. Conversely, for the nine months ended September 30, 2026, our AUM was negatively impacted by market value depreciation and net outflows from our precious metals products, partially offset by positive net inflows to our critical materials products. During the third quarter, we reported $207 million in net redemptions. On a year-to-date basis, net inflows were $1.1 billion as of September 30, 2026. Average AUM was $58.8 billion for the quarter, up $16.5 billion or 39% from $42.3 billion for the quarter ended September 30, 2025, and $64 billion on a year-to-date basis, up $26.3 billion or 70% from $37.7 billion for the nine months ended September 30, 2025. Subsequent to quarter-end, as at October 30, 2026, AUM was $52.1 billion.
Net income for the quarter was $22 million ($0.86 per share), up $8.8 million or 67% from $13.2 million ($0.51 per share) for the quarter ended September 30, 2025 and $85.5 million ($3.32 per share) on a year-to-date basis, up $46.9 million from $38.6 million ($1.50 per share) for the nine months ended September 30, 2025. Our net income performance was primarily due to higher average AUM in our exchange listed products and managed equities segments, as well as carried interest crystallization in our private strategies segment in the first quarter.
Adjusted EBITDA was $46 million ($1.79 per share) for the quarter, up $14.1 million or 44% from $31.9 million ($1.24 per share) for the quarter ended September 30, 2025 and $154.7 million ($6.01 per share) on a year-to-date basis, up $75.4 million or 95% from $79.3 million ($3.07 per share) for the nine months ended September 30, 2025. Our Adjusted EBITDA increased in the quarter and on a nine months ended basis due to higher average AUM, attributable to a combination of net inflows and market value appreciation across a majority of our fund products since the third quarter of last year, which more than offset the impact of year-to-date pullbacks in precious metals valuations.
Precious Metals
Precious metals recovered modestly during the third quarter of 2026, although significant volatility persisted. Spot gold rose 3.7% during the quarter to close September at US$4,157/oz, while silver gained 3.1% to US$60/oz. These quarterly gains masked a sharp September reversal, when gold declined 6.3% and silver fell 9.2% as rising real yields and a stronger U.S. dollar weighed on non-interest-bearing assets. The U.S. 10-year inflation-protected Treasury yield increased 49 basis points in September, highlighting the pressure from tighter financial conditions. Nevertheless, gold and silver finished the quarter above their June lows, while reported gold ETF holdings increased in September and silver ETF holdings rose for a third consecutive month. In our view, the quarter underscored the tension between near-term monetary headwinds and longer-term structural support. Rising sovereign debt burdens, persistent fiscal deficits and central bank reserve diversification continue to underpin gold’s role as a strategic monetary asset. Silver remains exposed to interest rate and currency volatility, but resilient Chinese demand and silver’s industrial applications provide additional support.
In recent weeks we have seen a long-overdue liquidity event begin to play out. Gold and silver are increasingly being caught in a broader liquidity-driven deleveraging cycle. As volatility rises, leveraged investors, systematic strategies and risk managers are forced to cut exposure, often selling liquid winners regardless of fundamentals. Gold may initially hold up better as falling bond yields support safe-haven demand, while silver’s higher volatility and industrial sensitivity leave it more exposed to forced liquidation. The result is a temporary disconnect from supportive fundamentals, with downside pressure likely to persist until positioning is sufficiently reduced and liquidity conditions stabilize.

Critical Materials
Uranium markets experienced a sharp contrast between improving fuel-market conditions and volatile mining equities during the third quarter of 2026. The uranium spot price increased during the quarter, and the long-term price reached an all-time high of US$96/lb, while uranium mining equities endured considerable volatility. In August, stronger pricing supported a rally in mining shares, with uranium miners up 17.2% before subsequent weakness erased those gains by quarter-end. In our view, the quarter’s central distinction was between the conditions underpinning uranium contracting and the financial-market pressures affecting mining shares. Utilities were accepting higher prices and more favorable producer terms to secure future supply, improving revenue visibility and mine economics even as equity markets remained volatile.
We remain constructive on the long-term outlook for critical materials. Discussions surrounding September’s World Nuclear Symposium reinforced the growing focus on financing, building and fueling additional nuclear capacity, supported by rising electricity demand, energy security priorities and the expansion of AI infrastructure. For uranium, declining utility contract coverage beyond 2030 and constrained mine supply strengthen the case for sustained investment in new production. Copper faces a similar challenge as grid expansion and electrification increase demand while declining ore grades and lengthy development timelines constrain supply growth. Copper prices also reached multiple all-time highs in the quarter, while copper miners were the standout critical materials subsector during both the quarter and year-to-date, reflecting growing recognition of these structural supply constraints. Efforts to diversify rare earth supply chains outside China add another important dimension to the opportunity. These structural drivers support our diversified critical materials platform and reinforce our constructive long-term outlook, even as commodity prices, financing conditions and investor flows are likely to remain volatile.
Physical Trusts
Our physical trust AUM increased by $1.2 billion during the quarter driven by somewhat higher gold and silver prices, offset by $416 million in redemptions from our precious metals trusts.
ETFs
We continued to see the benefits of our growing critical materials franchise during the third quarter with our critical materials ETFs delivering $236 million in net sales despite the volatile environment. On a nine-month basis, these strategies have accounted for $1.6 billion in net flows and we are actively exploring new ideas to expand this product suite.
Managed Equities
Precious metals equities rebounded during the third quarter, although September’s selloff underscored the sector’s continued volatility. Our flagship Sprott Gold Equity Fund returned 15.7% for the three months ended September 30, 2026. Our active ETFs continue to scale, with the combined AUM of the Sprott Active Gold & Silver Miners ETF (GBUG) and the Sprott Active Metals & Miners ETF (METL) exceeding $250 million.
Mining equities remain attractive with valuations that don’t currently reflect their stronger balance sheets and growing free cash flows.
Private Strategies
Our Private Strategies business continues to be well positioned, supported by favorable market tailwinds and a strong track record. This has driven increased demand on both sides of the platform, with borrowers and investees increasingly seeking our financing solutions and institutional investors looking to allocate additional capital to our strategies. The market continues to evolve toward less-dilutive financing solutions that preserve shareholder value and reduce long-term impacts on capital structures. This shift aligns closely with the products offered by Sprott’s Private Strategies team and is expected to support higher capital deployment in 2027 and beyond.

Outlook
Geopolitical conditions have worsened since our last letter. The war in Iran has re-escalated and is now a broader Middle East conflict with attacks on Saudi Arabia and many other Gulf states. Energy and other commodity prices are rising again with reserve stockpiles nearly depleted. The resultant inflationary expectations drove fixed income markets to their worst quarterly performance in decades. U.S. 10-year Treasuries declined by nearly 8% in the quarter with yields at levels not seen in two decades. This despite the best efforts of the U.S. Treasury Department and Federal Reserve to convince markets that they could control inflation and force rates back down. Federal Reserve chairman Kevin Warsh delivered a hawkish speech in August. Then in September, the Federal Reserve raised rates 25 basis points more to preserve credibility than to have an economic impact. For his part, Treasury Secretary Bessent directed an intervention in the currency markets to prevent Japan from selling U.S. Treasuries to defend its currency. Next, Bessent raised the Treasury's commitment to buy back long dated issues. And finally, he declared “I am the house” in an effort to scare short-term traders into covering their short positions. None of these moves had more than a momentary impact on halting the rise in rates. Further, describing the Department of the Treasury as some kind of casino can hardly inspire confidence. We are reminded of the famous quote from the 1975 movie Jaws, “You are going to need a bigger boat.” Perhaps that’s why Secretary Bessent recently hired sound money advocate Judy Shelton as a Counselor to the Treasury Department. Shelton’s responsibilities are said to include “advising Bessent on currency policy, with a particular focus on evaluating financial conditions in China.” While Shelton was widely ridiculed by the Senate when President Trump nominated her for a Federal Reserve position during his first term, her views about backing long-term Treasuries with some linkage to gold may not be so far fetched today. A quick study of China will reveal its continued gold purchases and the lowest 10-year interest rates of any major economy other than Switzerland.
On the surface, equity markets seemed more resilient with major U.S. indices hitting all-time highs and the S&P 500 index itself closing the third quarter just 2% off its all-time high. But once again this was driven by a handful of mega-cap technology companies benefiting from the perceived limitless growth in artificial intelligence. The mean performance of all stocks in the S&P 500 was negative 21%. In the broader Russell 3000 index, 57% of the stocks were down more than 20%. It appears to us that the generals have gotten too far ahead of their troops.
As the U.S. enters midterm elections, it seems that confidence in our leadership has deteriorated from an already low level. Nothing has been accomplished on the leading issues about the cost of living. In fact, the most recent news on inflation is heading in the wrong direction. The only tangible progress has been the renaming of some national monuments and bodies of water. The Gulf of Mexico is now the Gulf of America and Lake Ontario has become Lake America. We have heard that some Canadians prefer the name “The Strait of our Moose.” To be fair, there have also been some modest positives. We have made peace with Greenland and Maine Senator Susan Collins, who is locked in a tight Senate race, successfully lobbied the administration to lower trade tensions by reducing the 50% tariffs on some Canadian goods used by her state’s agricultural industry, including seaweed, rock salt and edible algae. Unfortunately for her constituents, these reductions will be offset by higher tariffs on shampoo, caskets, carpets and other household items.

We remain confident that the investment thesis for both precious metals and critical materials continues to improve. Our financial position remains strong and our free cash-flow generation is accelerating. We continue to exercise a disciplined capital allocation framework and on November 3, 2026 our board of directors approved a 25% increase to our regular quarterly dividend. During the year and through October we have bought back more than $20 million of our shares through our normal course issuer bid. And we have ample capacity to continue buying if market conditions cause the stock to trade below what we believe to be its long-term value.
As always, we thank you, our fellow shareholders, for your continued support. We look forward to reporting to you on our progress in the quarters ahead. We remain contrarian, innovative and aligned.

Key performance indicators and non-IFRS and other financial measures
The Company measures the success of its business using a number of key performance indicators that are not measurements in accordance with IFRS and should not be considered an alternative to net income (loss) or any other measure of performance under IFRS. Non-IFRS financial measures do not have a standardized meaning prescribed by IFRS and are therefore unlikely to be comparable to similar measures presented by other issuers. Our key performance indicators and non-IFRS and other financial measures are discussed below. For quantitative reconciliations of non-IFRS financial measures to their most directly comparable IFRS financial measures, please see page 12 of this MD&A.
Assets under management
Assets under management ("AUM") refers to the total net assets managed by the Company through its various investment product offerings and managed accounts.
Net inflows
Net inflows result in changes to AUM, and as such, have a direct impact on the revenues and earnings of the Company. They are described individually below:
Trust unit issuances and exchange traded funds ("ETF") unit ’creations’
The primary way in which inflows arise in our exchange listed products segment is through: (1) units of our physical trusts being issued through at-the-market (“ATM”) transactions, secondary public and private offerings; and (2) new 'creations' of ETF units.
Net sales
Fund sales (net of redemptions) are the primary manner in which net inflows arise in our managed equities segment.
Net capital calls
Capital calls, net of capital distributions ("net capital calls") are the primary manner in which net inflows arise in our private strategies segment.
Other net inflows
Other net inflows primarily include fund acquisitions. It is possible for committed capital in our private strategies to earn a commitment fee despite being uncalled, in which case, it will also be included in this category as AUM.
Net fees
Net fees are calculated as: (1) total management fees net of fund expenses and direct payouts; and (2) carried interest and performance fees, net of their related payouts. Net fees is a key revenue indicator as it represents revenue contributions after directly associated costs in managing our AUM.
Net commissions
Net commissions are calculated as total commissions, net of commission expenses. Net commissions primarily arise from the purchase and sale of critical materials in our exchange listed products segment.
Net revenues
Net revenues are calculated as the total of: (1) net fees, excluding carried interest and performance fees, net of their related payouts; (2) net commissions; (3) finance income; and (4) co-investment income.
Net compensation & net compensation ratio
Net compensation is calculated as total compensation expense before: (1) commission expenses paid to employees; (2) direct payouts to employees; (3) carried interest and performance fee payouts to employees; (4) severance and new hire accruals; and (5) impact of stock price changes and graded vesting amortization on cash-settled equity plans. Net compensation ratio is calculated as net compensation divided by net revenues.
Total shareholder return
Total shareholder return is the financial gain (loss) that results from a change in the Company's share price, plus any dividends paid over the period.
Liquid co-investments
Liquid co-investments are the Company's co-investments that can be monetized in less than 90 days.

EBITDA, adjusted EBITDA and adjusted EBITDA margin
EBITDA in its most basic form is defined as earnings before interest expense, income taxes, depreciation and amortization. Adjusted EBITDA further adjusts for items noted in the below reconciliation table. Adjusted EBITDA margin is calculated as adjusted EBITDA divided by net revenues.
EBITDA, adjusted EBITDA and adjusted EBITDA margin are measures commonly used in the investment industry by management, investors and investment analysts in understanding and comparing results by factoring out the impact of different financing methods, capital structures, amortization techniques and income tax rates between companies in the same industry. While other companies, investors or investment analysts may not utilize the same method of calculating EBITDA (or adjustments thereto), the Company believes its adjusted EBITDA metric results in a better comparison of the Company's underlying operations against its peers and a better indicator of recurring results from operations as compared to other non-IFRS financial measures. Adjusted EBITDA margin is a key indicator of the Company’s profitability on a per dollar of revenue basis, and as such, is commonly used in the financial services sector by analysts, investors and management.
Neither EBITDA, adjusted EBITDA or adjusted EBITDA margin have a standardized meaning under IFRS. Consequently, they should not be considered in isolation, nor should they be used in substitute for measures of performance prepared in accordance with IFRS.

Values associated with goodwill and intangibles involve estimates and assumptions, including those with respect to future cash inflows and outflows, discount rates, AUM and asset lives.
